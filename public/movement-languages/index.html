The Phrase — a LifeForms timeline

Cunningham’s digital notation, 1989–

LifeForms (Simon Fraser University; later DanceForms) represented choreography as keyframes on body-part channels — a wireframe figure with no weight, breath, or habit. Cunningham paired it with the chance operations he had used since the 1950s: cast the dice, accept the result, and let the body catch up to what the diagram proposes.

Each event sets one target for one channel: a direction, a level, a duration in beats, and a dynamic (Laban’s effort factors time and weight), which shapes the interpolation — sustained eases across the whole duration, sudden snaps early and holds.

B

Labanotation

Rudolf Laban, Kinetographie, 1928

A vertical staff read bottom to top. The staff’s columns name body parts; the shape of a symbol gives direction, its shading gives level (hatched = high, dot = middle, solid = low), and its length gives duration. Every parameter is spatial-temporal geometry first — the body is an address, not a picture.

Simplified staff — arm and leg gesture columns

Read upward from the double start line. Simplified here to four gesture columns (support columns omitted). In abstract mode the staff dissolves into Laban’s own deeper claim: direction is one of 26 rays of a point-centred lattice (the kinesphere), and duration is bare interval length.

C

Benesh Movement Notation

Rudolf & Joan Benesh, 1955

A five-line stave read left to right, like music — but the lines are a coordinate frame laid over the body seen from behind: top of head, shoulders, waist, knees, floor. Extremities are plotted where they appear in that frame; a stroke qualifier gives depth (| in front · — level · • behind). Each entry is a frame of film: notation as keyframing, thirty years before the software.

Stave — one frame per event

Rhythm marks above the stave give each frame’s duration in beats. In abstract mode the dancer vanishes and the parameter shows itself plainly: a point (x, y) in a picture plane plus a three-valued depth — a shallow 3-D coordinate.

D

Eshkol-Wachman Movement Notation

Noa Eshkol & Avraham Wachman, 1958

The body is treated as a chain of straight-line segments, each moving on the surface of a sphere centred at its joint. A position is just two numbers — a vertical and a horizontal coordinate in 45° units — and the score is a grid: rows for limbs, columns for time. It is the most abstract of the three by design, and has notated dance, sign language, and animal behaviour alike.

Score grid — rows × time units
System of reference — selected event

Coordinates are written vertical / horizontal: vertical 0 points straight down and 4 straight up; horizontal counts 45° units around the body (0 = forward). The sphere is the notation — in abstract mode nothing is removed but a label.

Cunningham and the digitized body

Merce Cunningham began working with LifeForms in 1989, in collaboration with the team led by Tom Calvert at Simon Fraser University; the software was later commercialized as DanceForms (Credo Interactive). Trackers (1991) was his first stage work choreographed with it. For BIPED (1999) he went further, working with the digital artists Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar to motion-capture dancers and project their hand-drawn, bodiless traces over the live stage. He valued the software precisely for its abstraction — it proposed articulations a trained body would never volunteer.

On the reduction

Each system is radically simplified here for comparison: one gesture per event, four limb channels, eight directions and three levels. Real Labanotation has support, body, and head columns and a large symbol repertory; real Benesh notates whole-body frames with locomotion below the stave; real Eshkol-Wachman subdivides angles as finely as needed and distinguishes plane, conical, and rotatory movement. The point of the reduction is the comparison: what remains constant when the spelling changes.

Method

A single shared data structure — {channel, direction, level, duration, dynamic} — drives every rendering on this page. That structure is itself the answer to the question the page asks: the abstracted parameter set, without a body.
