choreograph a body that isn’t there.

A working reconstruction of the ideas in LifeForms / DanceForms — the human-animation software Merce Cunningham composed with from 1989 until his death. Pose an articulated wireframe figure in the stance editor, set poses as keyframes on each dancer’s track, give the dancers floor paths, and let the machine interpolate what no rehearsal proposed. The dice are in the corner, where Cunningham kept them.

Every keyframe stores a full stance — ten segment directions in the same (azimuth, elevation) terms as the Parameters page — plus hip height, a floor position, and a facing. Between keyframes the figure is interpolated, which is the whole wager of the software: the transitions belong to the machine, and Cunningham liked to keep what it proposed.

Each view is a live translation of the same keyframes. The notations speak in whole limbs, so an edit made here sets the limb straight along the chosen direction — return to the stance editor to articulate elbow or knee. Laban and Benesh quantize what the stance editor states exactly; Eshkol-Wachman steps in its own 45° graduation. That asymmetry is the point: translation between movement languages is lossy in both directions.

The program

LifeForms began in the mid-1980s at Simon Fraser University, in the computer-graphics research group led by Tom Calvert, as a tool for composing human movement rather than animating characters. Cunningham started working with it in 1989, at first on a Macintosh in his studio, and made Trackers (1991) partly at the screen. The software was later developed and sold by Credo Interactive as DanceForms, which became a staple of choreography teaching. What drew Cunningham was not efficiency but estrangement: the figure had no habits, no training, and no fatigue, so its stances arrived without the body’s own censorship.

This reconstruction

Skeleton, stage, timeline, palette, paths, ghosting, and chance are all here in simplified form; the score persists in your browser’s local storage. The figure uses the same abstraction as the rest of this site — each segment is a direction on a sphere — so the studio and the notations are, deliberately, spellings of one another. See the Parameters page for the notation side.
